\section{Self-assessment}

\subsection{Our critique of our exam work}

\noindent \textbf{Achievements} \\ 
\noindent A relevant achievement of the project was the understanding we gained of the original TimeTrap work and of the timing mechanisms underlying its experiments. By studying the paper and the relevant parts of the ArduPilot and TurtleBot3 architectures, we were able to translate the high-level experimental description into concrete implementation and measurement choices. This process helped us distinguish the roles of targeted delay injection, resource profiling, and aggressor generation, and to identify which parts of the original methodology were reproduced, manually approximated, or left outside the scope of our work.

\noindent Another important achievement was the design of a structured experimental methodology. We defined architecture-specific baselines, repeatable execution procedures, source-level instrumentation, and quantitative metrics. We also distinguished internal timing measurements from externally observable control and mission indicators. These choices allowed us to compare the tested configurations under controlled conditions.

\noindent A significant achievement of the ArduPilot case study was the modification of the original SITL architecture to separate sensor emulation from the main control thread. This created two independently scheduled execution threads and made it possible to delay the control computation while the simulated physical system and sensor interfaces continued to progress.
Together with the custom instrumentation, this architecture allowed us to distinguish the availability of recent sensor updates from the age of the navigation state that was actually used by the waypoint controller. In the A-E3 configurations, the controlled delay produced a repeatable increase in processed-state age and a visible degradation of the simulated flight response. The experiment provided a concrete small-scale illustration of how a timing perturbation can generate an internal temporal misalignment and subsequently affect control behaviour.
An additional relevant observation was that all A-E3-50 runs completed the mission despite the intermediate deviations. This showed that mission completion alone is not sufficient to characterize nominal or safe behaviour. These findings apply to the selected SITL architecture, injection point, mission phase, and delay values; they support the investigated temporal-displacement mechanism but do not constitute a validation of the complete TimeTrap framework.
\\
A further achievement was the design of the TurtleBot3 case study across different stages of the ROS Navigation pipeline. By selecting injection points in localization, TF-publication, local-costmap production, and local-control execution, we were able to study not only the effect of increasing delays, but also the importance of where the perturbation was introduced. The zero-delay configurations also allowed us to verify that the instrumentation did not substantially alter nominal navigation behaviour. Another relevant achievement was the integration of source-level measurements with ROS bag analysis. The CSV logs described the internal timing effects of each suspension, while the ROS bags showed if those effects propagated to velocity-command timing, robot motion, and mission completion. This prevented us from treating every internal temporal displacement as an equivalent external failure. 
Finally, the study showed that mission success and the absence of a recorded collision were not sufficient indicators of nominal behaviour. Runs could still perform longer completion times, stopping, inefficient trajectories, recovery activity, or missing terminal results. Considering these intermediate effects allowed us to provide a more complete assessment of timing-related degradation than a simple success or failure classification.

\noindent \textbf{Limitations and failures} \\ 
\noindent This was our first experience reproducing a real-time systems research work. The main difficulty was translating a high-level research description into concrete and repeatable experiments. This required several methodological decisions concerning the software modifications, instrumentation, execution procedures, activation conditions, and evaluation metrics. Ensuring repeatability was also more difficult than expected. Small changes in the software architecture, timing instrumentation, or execution environment could alter the observed behaviour. So, a significant part of the project was dedicated to refining and validating the experimental method before answering the research questions.

\noindent Our experimental perimeter presents important limitations compared with the physical platforms evaluated by the authors. The original study included experiments on embedded hardware and physical autonomous systems, while our work used virtual machines together with SITL and Gazebo. These simulators reproduce the relevant software execution and selected vehicle dynamics, but they do not reproduce all mechanical, sensor, communication, actuation, and hardware-I/O effects of a physical platform. Virtualization also introduces an additional scheduling layer between guest threads, virtual CPUs, and the physical processor. Hence, host activity and hypervisor scheduling can affect wall-clock timing and reduce our control over the underlying processor and cache topology. Our results consequently describe the tested virtualized environments and cannot be directly generalized to physical ArduPilot or TurtleBot3 platforms.

\noindent The main unsuccessful aspect of the ArduPilot case study was that the low-priority cache-contention aggressor in A-E4 did not reproduce the timing and control effects observed with explicit delay injection. Its timing, state-age, and velocity-error measurements remained close to those of the matched A-E2 baseline. Virtualization is a plausible contributor to this negative result, but our measurements cannot establish it as the direct cause with certainty. VMware dynamically maps guest virtual CPUs onto physical processors, while only the aggressor was pinned to a guest vCPU. Moreover, we did not collect hardware performance counters, preventing us from determining if cache contention was absent or simply too weak to produce measurable effects. Therefore, we cannot conclude if the negative result reflects an ineffective interference strategy or insufficient control and observability of hardware resource contention in our experimental platform.
\\
The TurtleBot3 study was limited to a static simulated environment, one main navigation task, manually selected injection locations. Some configurations were also executed on virtual machines with different host processors. The static environment may also have made delayed localization or costmap information temporarily more usable than it would be in a dynamic scenario.
\\
The only concrete failure of the TurtleBot3 experimental procedure concerned the preliminary T-E2 attempts with 500-ms and 1000-ms TF-publication delays. In these attempts, the navigation task could not progress correctly from its initial phase: a valid destination could not be established and the robot did not visibly react. We cannot determine if the attempts were blocked by the injected delay, an unsuitable experimental strategy, an initialization or configuration error, or insufficient control of the virtualized ROS and Gazebo environment. These attempts were excluded from the analyzed T-E2 dataset and cannot be used as evidence of a robot stop or navigation failure caused by TF delay.

\subsection{Discussion of our learning outcomes}

\noindent ~\cite{T10}, ~\cite{T11}.

\noindent\textbf{L0: Relation between timing violations and control degradation} \\ %T01, T01-appr, T09
We consider this objective achieved inside the experimental scope of the project. In a feedback control loop (sensing-computing-actuating), the correctness of a real-time system belongs strictly to both the value and time domains. Our experiments provided a concrete manifestation of this principle: a computational result may be algorithmically correct, but it loses its utility if it is evaluated over stale data or actuated too late.
Through both case studies, we observed the practical development of the Dependability chain: \textit{Fault → Error → Failure}. The injected timing perturbation (\textit{Fault}) induced a temporal misalignment and a loss of data freshness within the system (\textit{Error}). When this internal anomaly propagated to the physical device, it caused the degradation of the navigation behaviour, acting as an observable deviation from the expected service (\textit{Failure}).
Specifically, in ArduPilot (configuration A-E3), we replicated a scenario closely related to the \textit{call-back problem}. While the high-priority sensor thread (\textit{producer}) continued to provide fresh updates, the delayed waypoint controller (\textit{consumer}) was forced to execute using an outdated navigation state. This temporal displacement directly compromised the control stability, resulting in severe velocity tracking errors.
In TurtleBot3, injecting delays at different stages of the execution pipeline showed how information ages before reaching the actuators. However, a crucial learning outcome was realizing that an internal timing error does not deterministically lead to a catastrophic external failure. As seen in some TurtleBot3 conditions (e.g. the AMCL pose-publication delay in T-E1), the internal temporal displacement increased; however, the robot maintained its path without crashing. From a dependability perspective, this demonstrates the inherent robustness of the control algorithms, which act as a form of graceful degradation able to absorb minor temporal displacements.

\noindent\textbf{L1: Controlled, repeatable, and measurable experimentation} \\ % T10, T01
We consider this objective achieved within the limits of our simulation-based environments. We defined explicit baseline configurations, repeatable execution procedures, and quantitative metrics for both internal timing effects and externally observable behaviour.
The TurtleBot3 case study also helped us understand the \textit{probe effect}: adding instrumentation can itself modify the timing behaviour of the program. For this reason, we ran the T-E0 experiment to compare it with the 0-ms baseline of the other configurations. This allowed us to understand the effect of introducing instrumentation in the source code.
The project also showed us that experimental repeatability is not the same as temporal predictability. Our case studies produced consistent measurements under the selected workloads and execution conditions. However, repeated tests and simulation cannot prove that all real-time requirements will be satisfied under every possible condition. The largest values recorded in our runs are only observed maxima; they are
not safe \textit{Worst-Case Execution Time} bounds. Therefore, our measurements are useful for comparing configurations and identifying timing-violation patterns, but they cannot be used as formal schedulability proofs or deadline guarantees.

\noindent\textbf{L2: Effects of different perturbations and injection locations} \\ % T11, T14, T08, T09
We consider this objective achieved. The experiments showed that the effect of a timing perturbation depends not only on its duration or resource demand, but also on how and where it is introduced into the system.
The ArduPilot case study allowed us to compare different perturbation mechanisms. Under the tested configuration, the wide resource-saturation workload in A-E1 did not produce a measurable change in the selected control metrics. In contrast, the targeted software suspension in A-E3 increased the age of the processed navigation state and degraded the simulated flight response. This comparison showed that a workload affecting the whole execution environment does not necessarily produce the same result as a delay placed at a specific point of the control path.
A-E4 provided an additional negative result. The low-priority cache workload did not reproduce the measurable timing and control effects observed in A-E3. Fixed-priority scheduling determines which runnable thread is selected when multiple threads compete for the same processor, but it does not directly regulate contention for resources shared across cores, such as caches and the memory system. However, A-E4 did not demonstrate this form of hardware interference. As discussed in the previous section, the negative result therefore cannot support wider conclusions about cache-based interference.
The TurtleBot3 case study showed that the injection location was also important. Delays in TF-publication and local-costmap production created measurable internal timing displacement but remained largely confined to those stages in the selected static scenario. Delays in pose-publication began to affect navigation only after becoming sufficiently large. In contrast, delays introduced directly in the local-control stages propagated more immediately to the \texttt{/cmd\_vel} stream and, at larger values, to motion continuity and mission completion. This helped us understand the concepts of \textit{producer-consumer} dependencies and \textit{end-to-end timing}: a local delay may propagate to later stages, but its effect depends on the position of the affected task and on how downstream components use the delayed data.

\noindent\textbf{L3: Propagation and containment of internal timing violations} \\ %T08, T09
We consider this objective achieved. We used the concepts of \textit{transactions},  \textit{precedence constraints}, and \textit{producer-consumer} dependencies as an interpretative model. They helped us interpret the two systems as execution pipelines in which the output of one stage becomes the input of another. Our experiments showed that a timing perturbation may affect later stages through increased data age, delayed delivery, or loss of periodicity. However, this propagation did not occur automatically or uniformly.
The TurtleBot3 experiments provided several examples of partial containment. Delays in TF-publication and local-costmap production produced clear internal timing displacement, but they did not substantially alter the \texttt{/cmd\_vel} stream or mission completion in the selected static environment. In these cases, downstream components could continue using previously available information that remained sufficiently representative of the environment. This result does not establish a general buffering or fault-containment property, but it shows that a large internal displacement does not necessarily produce an equivalent external effect. Delays in AMCL pose-publication also remained tolerated at lower values, while larger delays began to affect motion continuity and mission behaviour. Propagation was more direct when the perturbation was introduced in the local-control path. In T-E4 and T-E5, sufficiently large delays increased the intervals between consecutive \texttt{/cmd\_vel} messages and were associated with longer stops, degraded trajectories, and less reliable mission completion. 
Similarly, in ArduPilot A-E3, the waypoint controller used navigation state processed before the control-thread suspension, and the resulting increase in state age was associated with a degraded simulated flight response.
These observations helped us understand that an internal timing violation is not sufficient by itself to cause an externally visible control failure. Its propagation depends on the affected stage, the \textit{producer-consumer} relationships, the availability and usefulness of previous data, the magnitude of the perturbation, and the state of the operating environment. Therefore, \textit{end-to-end timing} behaviour cannot be inferred only from the delay measured at a single software component.

\noindent\textbf{L4: Critical evaluation of the reduced reproduction} \\ % T11
We consider this objective achieved within the scope of the project. The experiments allowed us to distinguish between the evidence provided by our results and the conclusions that the adopted methodology cannot support. In particular, the two case studies provided qualitative evidence that controlled timing perturbations can produce temporal displacement, that this displacement can affect control or navigation behaviour, and that its propagation depends on the affected stage and on the operating conditions.
At the same time, our work does not validate the complete TimeTrap framework. We manually selected the injection points and delay values, and most of the perturbations were implemented as explicit software suspensions.
Simulation and virtualization introduced further limitations. 
In particular, in multicore environments, tasks executing on different cores may still interfere through shared resources such as caches, memory controllers, and memory buses. This interference can introduce stalling and makes compositional timing analysis more difficult. However, the A-E4 experiment did not directly demonstrate this mechanism, as explained in the previous section. Therefore, the negative A-E4 result only describes the selected workload and environment; it does not establish that cache interference would be ineffective on a physical platform.
Our conclusions are limited to the tested configurations, workloads, missions, and virtualized platforms. The results are useful for comparing perturbation mechanisms and studying timing propagation, but they cannot be generalized directly to physical CPS platforms. They also do not constitute a formal schedulability analysis, a validation of TimeTrap, or a complete safety assessment.
